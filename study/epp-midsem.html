Sheet 01 · Module I · Lecture 1

Engineering as a Discipline and a Profession

Definition — learn verbatim

Engineering is a professional discipline that combines knowledge from physics, mathematics and material science with innovation, analysis and design to develop technologies and solutions that meet human needs in a safe, efficient and sustainable way.

Engineering as a discipline

A branch of academic study and systematic knowledge that applies scientific and mathematical principles to solve real-world problems. It is about generating knowledge: learning theories, developing technologies, designing systems and innovating.

Characteristics

  • Interdisciplinary in nature
  • Strong foundation in mathematics and science
  • Involves design, analysis and experimentation
  • Emphasis on continual improvement and lifelong learning

Six key components (a common 5-mark question)

  1. Scientific foundation — laws of nature: how forces act, energy flows, materials behave.
  2. Mathematical modelling — mathematics is the language of engineering; equations model systems, predict outcomes and optimise performance.
  3. Design thinking — define a problem, brainstorm, model and simulate, improve through iteration.
  4. Technical knowledge — each branch has its own technical core.
  5. Experimentation and analysis — labs validate theory; collect data, analyse results.
  6. Innovation and research — new materials, energy-efficient systems, better processes.

Engineering as a profession

The practical application of engineering knowledge to design, develop, operate and maintain systems, products and processes that serve society — done responsibly, ethically and efficiently.

Six key elements

  1. Application of knowledge — e.g. a mechanical engineer designs an energy-efficient HVAC system for a hospital.
  2. Professional responsibility — decisions affect people, property and environment; engineers are trusted to act in the public interest.
  3. Ethics and integrity — honesty, accountability, fairness; safety over profit. An engineer must refuse to certify a faulty design even under management pressure.
  4. Licence and certification — in India, a Chartered Engineer (CE) registered with the Institution of Engineers (India), IEI, signs off government work for CPWD, Indian Railways or NHAI. In the USA, a Professional Engineer (PE) licence is needed to approve public structural designs.
  5. Teamwork and communication — multidisciplinary work with architects, doctors, clients, government.
  6. Lifelong learning and innovation — e.g. an electrical engineer learning AI and IoT for smart grids.

Discipline vs profession — reproduce this table

AspectAs a disciplineAs a profession
Focus & practiceGeneration of knowledge, theories, innovation and researchPractical application of knowledge to solve real-world problems
SettingAcademic institutions, research labs, universitiesIndustries, corporate sector, consultancy, government agencies
OutputScientific theories, experimental results, conceptual designsFunctional products, infrastructure, processes, services
GoalAdvance the frontiers of science and technologyEnsure safety, efficiency and service to society

Closing line worth memorising: engineering is a discipline grounded in science and a profession rooted in service — it bridges theory and practice.

Sheet 02 · Module I · Lecture 2

The Practising Engineer

Who is a practising engineer

Someone who applies engineering principles to solve real-world problems and is actively involved in the engineering profession. This may be a licensed Professional Engineer (PE) offering services to the public, or someone gaining experience under the supervision of a PE.

Key attributes

  • Technical proficiency — mastery of core principles
  • Ethical integrity — codes of ethics, professional conduct
  • Problem-solving skills — analytical and innovative mindset
  • Communication skills — effective oral and written
  • Teamwork and collaboration — multidisciplinary teams
  • Adaptability and lifelong learning

Additional attributes

  • Project management skills — planning, executing, leading
  • Decision-making under constraints — balancing cost, time, quality, safety
  • Social and environmental responsibility — sustainability and societal impact
  • Leadership qualities — influencing, guiding, mentoring

Four core functions

  1. Design and development — creating products, processes or systems
  2. Analysis and testing — evaluating performance, ensuring quality
  3. Implementation and operation — overseeing production and daily functions
  4. Maintenance and support — ensuring long-term functionality

The engineer's three roles

1 · As a problem solver

The engineer defines the objective and constraints, generates alternatives, analyses them and selects the workable solution — moving a problem from an ill-defined statement to a specified, buildable answer.

2 · As a designer

The engineering design process is a series of steps engineers follow to find a solution to a problem: determining objectives and constraints, then prototyping, testing and evaluation — iterating until the design satisfies the need.

3 · As a change agent

A change agent identifies areas for improvement, initiates innovation, implements new solutions, and influences others toward positive transformation.

Sheet 03 · Module II · Lecture 3

Designing for Safety

Definition — learn verbatim

Safety is the condition of being protected from harm, danger or injury. In engineering it is the practice of designing systems, processes and products so as to minimise risk to human life, property and the environment during both normal operation and failure conditions.

Design for Safety is the application of principles, criteria and techniques of engineering and management to optimise all aspects of security within the constraints of operational efficiency, time and cost, in all phases of the life cycle of a system. It is a planned, disciplined and systematic approach to prevent or reduce accidents throughout that life cycle.

Historical note worth one mark: Design for Safety first gained importance in the 1950s, driven by the United States Air Force, which concluded that designing aircraft by trial and error and continuous improvement would never be viable. From this came the MIL-STD-882 standard, still in force today.

Three basic principles

  1. Safety begins in the early stages of design, development, testing and design review. Hazard identification processes must be established so hazards can be controlled by changing the design. The sooner weaknesses are analysed, the easier, faster and cheaper the change is.
  2. Learn from the deficiencies of previous designs to avoid their recurrence. Without applying lessons learned, a design never advances — it always starts from the same point.
  3. Safety requires engineering and management techniques to control hazards. A safety programme must be planned and implemented so safety analyses are integrated with the other factors that impact management decisions.

Eight key considerations

ConsiderationWhat it means
Risk assessmentIdentify hazards and evaluate possible dangers and their consequences
User training & educationClear, comprehensive material so the product is used properly
ErgonomicsComfortable, efficient, user-friendly design; reduces musculoskeletal disorders
Redundancy & fail-safesCritical systems still function safely if the primary system fails
AccessibilityUsable by people of all abilities; compliance with accessibility standards
Material selectionSafe for user and environment; no harmful chemicals or fire threat
Human–machine interfaceIntuitive, clear instructions, safeguards against user error
Product life-cycleSafety across design, manufacturing, use and disposal

Three factors of safety in engineering

1 · Hardware safety

  • Electrical protection — avoid short circuits, overheating, fires (fuses, thermal cutoffs)
  • EMI shielding — prevent electromagnetic interference
  • Safe power supply design — stable voltage and isolation to avoid shocks
  • Redundant components — e.g. dual power supplies in servers

2 · Software safety

  • Safe coding practices — prevent buffer overflows, race conditions, memory leaks
  • Exception handling — handle sensor failure without dangerous results
  • RTOS — time-bound, predictable behaviour for medical devices, automotive ECUs
  • Fail-safe defaults — default to a safe state on error, e.g. stopping a robot arm

3 · Cyber-physical safety

  • Secure communication — prevent unauthorised control (hacked pacemakers, industrial PLCs)
  • Sensor validation — cross-verify inputs so bad data cannot drive unsafe action
  • User interface design — clear alerts, confirmation for critical actions

Three safety analysis tools — a near-certain question

ToolDirectionWhat it does
FMEA
Failure Mode and Effects Analysis		Bottom-up, inductiveSystematically identifies potential failures in a system, process or product and assesses their severity, likelihood and detectability — proactively addressing problems before they occur
FTA
Fault Tree Analysis		Top-down, deductiveStarts from an undesired top event and maps its contributing causes using a diagram of events and logic gates, identifying root causes
HAZOP

Memory hook: FMEA asks "if this part fails, what happens?" (cause → effect). FTA asks "this disaster happened — what could have caused it?" (effect → cause). HAZOP asks "what if this process deviates from design intent?"

Sheet 04 · Module II · Lecture 4

Designing for Reliability

Definition — learn verbatim

Reliability is the probability that a product, system or service will perform its intended function adequately for a specified period of time, or will operate in a defined environment without failure.

Note the four examinable ingredients in that sentence: it is a probability, tied to an intended function, over a stated time, under stated conditions. Dropping any one of them loses marks.

Why design for reliability

  • Enhances customer satisfaction
  • Reduces warranty costs
  • Improves brand reputation
  • Reduces total life-cycle cost
  • Ensures safety and compliance

Reliability across the product life cycle

PhaseReliability activity
ConceptRequirements specification
DesignFMEA, fault-tolerant design
ManufacturingProcess capability, quality control
OperationMaintenance strategy, failure analysis

Six design techniques for reliability

  1. Redundancy — duplication of critical components or functions so the system continues operating if one part fails.
  2. Derating — operating electrical and electronic devices at less than their maximum rated capacity to improve reliability and lifespan.
  3. Environmental protection — e.g. conformal coating against moisture.
  4. Stress–strength analysis — assessing probability of failure by comparing applied stress to inherent strength.
  5. Modular design — subdividing a system into smaller, independent, interchangeable modules, each performing a specific function.
  6. Robust design (Taguchi methods) — creating products that perform consistently well despite variation in manufacturing, usage or environment.

Quality vs reliability — reproduce this table

AspectQualityReliability
TimeframeAt the time of purchase / useOver a period of time
MeasureMeets specifications and expectationsPerforms consistently without failure
ExampleA sharp new LED TV with vibrant coloursA washing machine that works well for 10 years
FocusInitial features and performanceLong-term dependability

One-line answer: quality is how a product performs initially; reliability is how it continues to perform over time. Reliability is quality extended over time.

Four reliability tests

  • ALT — Accelerated Life Testing
  • HALT — Highly Accelerated Life Testing
  • Burn-in testing — weeds out infant-mortality failures before shipping
  • ESS — Environmental Stress Screening

Two case studies the deck uses

Case · Automotive ECU design

High-vibration environment. Conformal coating for moisture resistance; redundant power supplies for fail-safe operation; MTBF target 100,000 hours. FMEA revealed thermal stress as the major issue → improved heat sinks.

Case · Smartphone battery reliability

Frequent customer complaints of overheating. Root cause: charging circuit component failure. Solution: improved thermal pad placement plus component derating. Outcome: MTBF improved by 45%.

The line the deck closes on, and a perfect concluding sentence: "Reliability is designed, not tested in." Tools like FMEA and FTA identify and mitigate failure risks; robust design ensures long-term performance and customer trust.

Sheet 05 · Module II · Numerical

Reliability Mathematics

Reconstructed contentSlides 15–19 of the reliability deck are titled "Reliability Calculation" but are images, so no text could be extracted from them. This sheet rebuilds the standard treatment those slides almost certainly carry. Check the worked forms against your class notes before relying on the exact style your examiner wants.

The four core quantities

λ = number of failures / total operating timeFailure rate. Units: failures per hour. Constant during useful life.
R(t) = e−λtReliability function for a constant failure rate (exponential distribution). Probability of surviving to time t.
F(t) = 1 − R(t) = 1 − e−λtUnreliability — probability that failure occurs by time t.
MTBF = 1 / λ MTTF = 1 / λMTBF for repairable items (mean time between failures); MTTF for non-repairable items (mean time to failure). Distinguishing these two is a classic one-mark trap.
Availability A = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR)MTTR = mean time to repair. Availability is the fraction of time the system is up.

The bathtub curve

System reliability — series and parallel

Series: Rs = R₁ · R₂ · … · Rn λs = λ₁ + λ₂ + … + λn
Parallel: Rp = 1 − (1−R₁)(1−R₂) … (1−Rn)

Worked numericals

W1 · Exponential reliability and MTBF

A component has a constant failure rate λ = 2 × 10⁻⁴ failures/hour. Find its reliability for a 1,000-hour mission, and its MTBF.

R(1000) = e^(−2×10⁻⁴ × 1000) = e^(−0.2) = 0.8187 MTBF = 1/λ = 1/(2×10⁻⁴) = 5,000 hours

R = 81.87%, MTBF = 5,000 h

W2 · Series system

Three components with reliabilities 0.95, 0.92 and 0.98 are connected in series. Find system reliability.

Rs = 0.95 × 0.92 × 0.98 = 0.8565

Rs = 85.65% — note it is below the worst component (0.92).

W3 · Parallel redundancy

Two identical units, each of reliability 0.90, are placed in parallel. Find system reliability.

Rp = 1 − (1−0.90)(1−0.90) = 1 − (0.10)(0.10) = 1 − 0.01

Rp = 0.99 = 99% — one redundant unit turns 90% into 99%.

W4 · Series–parallel combination

A unit of reliability 0.90 is in series with a parallel pair each of reliability 0.80. Find system reliability.

Parallel pair: Rp = 1 − (0.2)(0.2) = 0.96 Whole system: Rs = 0.90 × 0.96 = 0.864

R = 86.4% — always collapse parallel blocks first, then multiply along the series.

W5 · MTBF from test data, and availability

Ten units are tested for 500 hours each; 4 failures occur. Repairs average 50 hours. Find λ, MTBF and availability.

Total operating time = 10 × 500 = 5,000 unit-hours MTBF = 5,000 / 4 = 1,250 hours λ = 1/1250 = 8 × 10⁻⁴ failures/hour A = MTBF/(MTBF+MTTR) = 1250/(1250+50) = 1250/1300

MTBF = 1,250 h, A = 96.15%
